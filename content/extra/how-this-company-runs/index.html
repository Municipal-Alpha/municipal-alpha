What the company is, in five minutes, because it is not the subject

The problem

Municipal records are scattered across thousands of separate systems, so getting intelligence out of them is very hard.

How do I solve it?

Aggregate it, normalise it, and build a graph, so you can ask a question about municipal government and get an answer.

979,681 documents across 4,895 municipalities, crawled nightly.

Who pays?

Two live customers, a newspaper group and an economic development organisation. Both four figures a year, and that is the small end on purpose.

Six thousand six hundred contracted, first signed in June. Those two are newsrooms and a non-profit, which is where I started because they would tell me the truth about the product.

The commercial buyers in the pipeline are a different order. The proposal out right now converts a free six-week calibration to three thousand a month, and the segments I have sized sit between five and low six figures a year depending on how deeply we read their towns. One of those buyers is in conversation at that scale, not paying yet.

Who else would?

Any business where a decision made in municipal government hits their bottom line.

Newsrooms, engineering and construction, people selling software into towns, bond analysts, developers. Different questions, one graph.

Is anyone else doing this?

Yes. Starbridge raised fifty-two million, and there are others.

They built the full commercial surface, CRM sync, a browser extension, an RFP writer, buyer attributes joined out of federal datasets. That is a real product and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

And I can compete?

Yes, with the system I am about to walk you through.

Founder capital and two people, so competing on surface area is not available. Reading the primary record deeply enough to find what a joined dataset cannot contain is, and depth per document at two people is an automation problem.

What lands

An email, weekly, per customer. No dashboard, no search box, nobody logs in.

Answers Q1 and Q3

The company runs on an operating procedure that is written down, versioned and enforced, the same as its code

People hear "AI company" and think of two things, AI in the product or AI tools for the team, and this is neither.

How this company operates is written as instructions a machine reads before it does anything, versioned beside the product, and enforced where it matters. Not in my head, not in a wiki.

So the question is not whether the model does good work. It is what a company looks like when its rules have to be legible to something that forgets everything between sessions.

Four tiers. A practice, a rule, a principle, a commandment, and the gates that give exactly one of those tiers teeth. Nothing moves up a tier for being a good idea. A practice becomes a rule on the day it fails and the failure costs something real, a fabricated claim reaching my own website or an email going out broken to a customer, and from that day the rule carries the date and the incident that bought it. The rest of this block walks down them in order.

First How anything gets onto the ladder without me writing it down

Wikis get stale and intranets go cold, because both need somebody to stop working and go file something. So nothing here asks me to. The capture is a byproduct of work that was going to happen anyway.

Tier 1 A practice is something that worked once

Written down, no date, and nothing enforces it. Three hundred and forty-seven of them.

  • Price the aim, not the effort. Lead with their pain, neutrally, and pivot only once they answer.
    • Both are commercial judgment that cost me something to learn, both are loaded before anything gets drafted, and if the system ignores one, nothing happens. No check, no gate, no consequence.
  • A failure is not a tier on this ladder. It is the thing that pushes one practice up.

The day a practice fails and the failure costs something, it gets a date. That is the whole promotion.

Tier 2 A rule is a practice that failed, with the date still on it

Twenty-nine of them load before I write a line. Every one names the incident that bought it, so I can argue with the rule by arguing with the incident. Here are five.

Q1 The judgment I made over and over and never wrote down

  • Should a new rule stop me, or just tell me?
    • I decided that case by case for months and never wrote it down, so I got it wrong in both directions. Rules that should have blocked only warned, and warnings I could not skip blocked work worth doing.
  • It broke in May. A rule I proposed would have blocked three of the best sessions this company has had.
  • Now it is one sentence, and it decides the shape of everything written since.

"If I want to do this anyway, should the system stop me?" Yes means it fails closed. No means it shows the cost and gets out of the way.

"Founders need guardrails, and pushback, but when they push through those pushbacks, sometimes you get the novel stuff."

And how twenty-nine stays twenty-nine

  • They get eaten by better ones. When several incidents turn out to be one shape, the shape replaces them rather than joining them.

Tier 3 A principle is what four rules turn out to have been all along

The box at the bottom of that diagram is a principle. Nobody sat down to write it. It was the only thing five unrelated incidents had in common.

  • It covers cases no rule was written for, which is the entire point.
    • Five rules would each have named one subsystem. The principle names the shape, so it fires on the sixth case nobody had seen yet.
  • A principle cannot have a gate. It works by being loaded before I write anything, not by refusing after.
  • Neither of these was written on purpose. Both are what was left over after several rules turned out to be saying the same thing in different subsystems.
Principle one · what five incidents had in common

A mechanism whose healthy output looks exactly like its broken output needs something attached to it that can go red.

The box at the bottom of the diagram above. It now fires on shapes none of those five rules would have named, which is the whole reason it is worth more than the five.

What it actually says

The test. Of any guard, gate, filter, detector or probe: if this were broken right now, what would its output look like? If the answer is "the same as when it is working", it needs a witness.

Three states, and all three print nothing. Ran and found nothing, which is genuine health. Ran but examined nothing, meaning wrong population or empty query. Did not run at all. Only the first is good news.

A witness is a count of what it examined, kept across runs, that can drop. Read it as a trend, not a zero check: four thousand falling to three thousand two hundred never trips an alarm and is exactly how the detector that produced this rule failed.

Principle two · what this entire block is built on

Enforcement is the substance of a rule. The text is only the specification of the enforcement.

A rule nothing enforces is a reminder, and reminders decay. Every gate further down this page descends from it, and it is the closest thing here to a thirteenth commandment.

What it actually says

The test. If this rule were violated right now, would any tool catch it? Yes and it fails closed is load-bearing. Yes but it only warns is partial. No, only a human would notice, is advisory.

The correction is never "be more careful." It is "why didn't the tool catch this, and what needs to change." If the answer is that the tool would have to be built, decide whether to build it or admit the rule is advisory and mark it so.

The fork. Block when I could not reliably catch it myself. Surface and get out of the way when pushing through is the point, because a judgment rule made mechanical removes the override that produces the interesting work.

One of those five incidents, made concrete.

Tier 4 Twelve commandments, and they exist to break ties

When two rules disagree, something has to settle it. These do. They are also what a novel situation gets read against when no rule applies at all.

  • Changing one takes a written argument and a review by a different model. That expense is deliberate. A tie-breaker I can edit casually is not a tie-breaker.
  1. IEvery claim traces to a source.
  2. IIGuard the trust.
  3. IIIThe system runs without you.
  4. IVMarket signal outranks internal signal.
  5. VShip, then compound.
  6. VIMatch the weight of the solution to the weight of the problem.
  7. VIIDiagnose before you prescribe.
  8. VIIIFix at the most general level the evidence supports, but no further.
  9. IXBe wrong fast.
  10. XCapture what you learn or lose it.
  11. XIEvery repeated judgment is a failure of encoding.
  12. XIIThe work compounds.
  • Eleven is the one this whole hour is about. If I am still making a decision the system should be making, that is a missing rule, not a necessary human input.
IEvery claim traces to a source.

If it leaves the building, it is verified or it is labelled. No exceptions for "illustrative" or "realistic." Verify before you act on assumptions, about data, about system state, about what day it is.

What it settles. Whether a number can be said out loud. Whether a second model has to read something before it ships. Whether a status comes from the system or from a document describing the system. Whether the day of the week gets checked before something dated goes out. Seven separate rules answer to it, and when two of them disagree this is what breaks the tie.

This page is an instance of it. Everything on it traces to a file, a record or a transcript. Where something could not be traced, it came off the page rather than being softened.

Answers Q3

The failure that produced the loop, and it ran again yesterday

A system cannot check its own work. That is the failure worth telling, because the fix is the only thing on this page that catches what no gate can.

  • Twenty outbound emails, checked by the layer that wrote them, reported nineteen of nineteen clean. An independent check found five fabrications, and the self-check had scored those five highest.
    • It did not miss the errors so much as launder them. A rumour being knocked down and a live debate read identically to something checking for keywords, so confidence ran highest on the worst ones.
  • So now a second model attacks the work, and it is pointed at the assumption rather than the code. Here is what that did yesterday.

The recording below is one of these sessions, unedited except for length and redaction. It opens on a check that reported zero results and no error, which is the ambiguous case this whole page is about: nothing found and nothing broken look identical. It was the check that was broken, not the inbox. What follows is the repair, the witness added so the same silence cannot happen twice, and a moment near the end where the second model refuses a claim I had already written down.

Eleven and a half minutes, no audio, captions burned in. Four fast-forwarded stretches where agents are working in parallel and there is nothing to read.

  • I wrote a rule that pulled dates out of filenames. It looked obviously right. The reviewer would not argue about it, it told me to go measure it first.
  • The measurement killed my own idea. Twelve percent of rows came back rewritten, and the biggest ones were all wrong.
  • Narrowed to one case, re-measured, thirty-two out of thirty-two. That version shipped. The first version would have quietly corrupted dates.

A fair reading is that I described an elaborate system then told you how it failed last week. Correct reading, wrong objection. The failures do not stop, each one becomes something that refuses next time, so it costs once rather than every time.

This is how a rule gets built, but only if the failure gets captured and codified into something that acts. A failure that produced a wiser person produced nothing, because the wiser person forgets or leaves.

The point is not that I stop making mistakes. It is that I stop making these ones.

Somebody asked what I would want measured before believing any of this works. I could produce retention and conversion by Friday and every number would be decoration, because I have two customers and both are months old. The measurement that means something is the near-miss rate, and yesterday it was two.

OK Christian, where did I miss the mark?

Christian joined on the sixth of August doing go-to-market. Every rule you just saw was written by me, for me, and he walked into it cold. He is also the answer to my own question two, because most of what waits on one person around here waits on me.

Before I hand it to all of you, I want you to hear somebody other than me answer these.

Six topics to put to him, and the last one is the one I most want answered in front of the room.

Legibility

What were you deciding over and over, on artifact after artifact, that nobody had written down?

His is legibility. "If I, as a new partner, do not understand what is being said, a prospect never will." He made that call on artifact after artifact until it became a written rule that now binds me too.

Where you wait on me

Where does your work stop and wait for me, and how long does it usually sit there?

This is the one only he can answer honestly, because the answer is me. Database writes, data pulls, a credential he asked for and waited on, approvals. He has a list and I would rather it were said out loud than summarised by me.

Speaking for the company

The voice review graded every draft he wrote against a spec mined from my transcripts, so it kept telling him to sound like me until that was fixed. Two always-loaded rules told him never to use the only mail path he has.

Why join this

He described himself, unprompted, as "your non-AI voice trying to encode itself into the system." That is a better answer than mine and it is his to give.

Where we fall short

Where are we not actually doing what these rules say we do?

The most useful thing he can say in four minutes. A second operator who inherited the rules is the only person positioned to say which of them are theatre.

What a second person actually changes, as a mechanism rather than a sentiment. The other thing a second operator settles is which parts of this are the company and which parts are just me. A rule that is right for me and wrong for him is worse than no rule, because it loads in his sessions too.

Who writes the code, and whether this is just vibe coding

The agent system writes essentially all of it. The difference between that and vibe coding is not how much I read, it is what has to be true before anything lands.

  • Vibe coding is accepting output you have no way to evaluate. Everything below exists to make the output evaluable without me reading every line, because reading every line does not scale to two people and would not catch the failures that actually happen anyway.
The questionWhat is actually there
Do you write PRDs? No. There are 149 written plans, but only for the class of change whose mistakes compound, and each carries a field saying whether a second model reviewed it. 109 say passed, with the findings and my disposition of each written into the plan. Ordinary changes get no plan at all.
Where is the spec? It is a completion contract, one per kind of change rather than one per feature, which is why it does not rot. Twenty of them. A code change is not done until every modified file parses, imports are updated, the smoke tests pass and the decision is written down. A parser change adds two data-integrity gates. A new gate has to be shown failing on the real defect first.
Test-driven? Not in the usual sense, and stricter where it counts. 5,786 tests across 302 files, but the rule is not coverage. A test is evidence only if it could have failed on the real defect, and only about the thing it actually examined. That came from one day with four passing checks that each proved nothing.
What stops bad code landing? Twenty-six checks before a commit, more before a push, then CI. CI is the one that matters because it does not run on my machine, so it is the only one I cannot skip in a hurry.
Where do skills fit? Nine, and they are the archetype layer. A job I would otherwise re-explain every time becomes a named procedure the system loads when the work matches: onboard a town, triage a records response, prep for a buyer call. Not code and not a prompt, a written-down way of doing a recurring job.
Why not use an off-the-shelf agent framework? The harness is off the shelf. I did not build the thing that writes code, and I would not. What no framework ships is the part that makes it trustworthy here, because every rule in it is dated to an incident that happened to this company. You can copy the shape, which is what this hour is. You cannot buy the contents, and a corpus of somebody else's incidents would be worse than none.
Is this Claude-specific? The harness is. Almost none of the enforcement is. Twenty-six commit checks, a CI workflow, two push gates and thirty-seven refusal functions all run in git and Python, and none of them knows or cares what wrote the code. What is Claude-specific is how thirty rule files get loaded into context, which is a file convention, not a mechanism. Move harness and that is the part I would rewrite; everything that actually refuses would carry over untouched. That split is deliberate. A gate that lives inside the agent's context can be forgotten, argued with, or lost when the context resets. A gate in a commit hook cannot. And the adversarial reviewer is deliberately a different vendor's model, because a second opinion from the same family is not a second opinion.
How did you design it? I did not. Every row above was bought by something going wrong, which is the same answer as the rest of this page and the reason I trust it more than a design I had reasoned out in advance.
  • The shape of the work matters here, and that shape was a decision rather than luck. No dashboard, no search box, nobody logs in, an email arrives. I scoped it that way on purpose, because an interface is the rat hole I am most prone to and it would have eaten the year without producing a single signal. What that choice also bought is a system with no component library, no state across screens, no accessibility surface, none of the interaction complexity that makes most software hard to verify. Some of what makes code hard to trust, I chose not to have.
  • The other limit is that none of this makes the code good, it makes it checkable. A bad decision that passes every gate still ships. What the gates buy is that the same bad decision does not ship twice.
