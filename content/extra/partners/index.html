Founding Partners

I built a company that runs on one person and an encoded operating system. It's selling. There are a few seats, for a specific kind of person.

This isn't a "come be my cofounder and help me build the thing" page, the thing is built, it has closed customers, pilots in flight priced to convert to full rate without renegotiation, and a qualified market far larger than one person can cover. And the economics are why the partnership math works: infrastructure cost scales with coverage, a finite number of US municipalities, not with revenue, and serving the next customer costs close to nothing, so growth lands as margin, and in this model margin becomes annual profit distributions to the people who hold a stake. This is not a low-margin business with equity sprinkled on top. It runs on zero outside capital and it is designed to run without any one person, including me. So this isn't an "I need you" pitch, it's simpler than that. Here is what I'm doing, here is the evidence it works, and if you can bring something the system doesn't have, you should be interested.

And if you're sitting inside a towers, solar, battery, energy, REIT, private equity, gov-adjacent, or engineering firm right now, frustrated that your company isn't embracing what's obviously coming, and you can articulate exactly how you'd meet the future instead, this page was written for you specifically. That's one shape of the right person, not the boundary. The domains can be absorbed, the system helps with that part. What can't be taught is seeing the future clearly enough to articulate it, and wanting to build toward it instead of waiting for permission.

What this company is

A rural county quietly amends its solar ordinance. A small town budgets for outside counsel on a contamination fight. A state bill starts moving that rewrites where public notices must be published. Decisions like these move real projects and real dollars, they sit in the public record of thousands of US municipalities months before they surface anywhere else, and almost nobody is reading. Municipal Alpha reads all of it, across all 50 states, and puts what matters in front of the buyers it affects, in infrastructure, energy, finance, and engineering.

The company is early revenue, growing, and deliberately bootstrapped. No investors, no board, no plan to sell. The model is a professional partnership, closer to a law firm than a startup, and the return is a share of profit, every year, from a durable private company. If you are optimizing for a lottery-ticket exit, this is the wrong page.

How it's built, which is the part that surprises people

One person plus an encoded operating system. The pipeline that reads the record runs itself. The judgment layer, how I price, how I qualify, how I write, what I learned from every buyer call, lives in written rules and playbooks that an AI operating layer executes and improves every day. I have been the only human in the building while the system does work that normally takes a team.

That changes what a partner is here. This isn't traditional commercial sales, it isn't SaaS quota carrying, and it isn't "own your book." You bring the one domain where you beat everyone, your spike, and the system carries you at competence everywhere else. What you know gets encoded into the machine, where it compounds for every partner and every customer, instead of living in your head and walking out with you. Partners are guides for the machine, not gears in it, and the rewards are sized for the person who amplifies the system, not just adds to it. Contributing is table stakes. The seat pays for compounding.

The strange consequence, stated honestly. Your advancement here is earned partly by encoding what you know into the system, so your function runs even when you're absent. At a normal company, being irreplaceable is your leverage. Here it's inverted, on purpose, in writing. Making yourself replaceable is how you get paid more, promoted, and eventually bought out. If that inversion reads as interesting rather than threatening, keep going.

Who the fuck is this guy, and why believe him?

Most recently I was Chief Product Officer at Acast, podcast infrastructure, a $20M P&L, a 150-plus person product, engineering, and design org, $200M in annual revenue across 13 markets, through a NASDAQ IPO. Before that I co-founded RadioPublic, raised $3.5M, built the team from zero, and took it to acquisition, by Acast. Before that I was CPO at PRX, where we built the apps for This American Life, Radiolab, and The Moth. And before any of that I co-founded the first version of this exact company: NearbyFYI, in 2012, machine learning on municipal documents, a Knight Foundation News Challenge semi-finalist. It ended in 2014. This is my second run at this exact problem, fourteen years and one AI revolution apart.

The distrust case, because past performance is not indicative of future results. Those wins came with teams, capital, and timing that this doesn't have, this is one person betting the leverage equation changed. I came at this problem once before and the first run didn't become a durable business, discount me accordingly. What I'll claim for myself is only this: I think it's the best concept, problem, and solution I have worked on so far, and this time the tools exist. Check me, the history is public, ask people who worked with me, or paste this page into your AI and ask what to verify. "Trust me" isn't the pitch. The written-down model and the evidence are.

The seats the system needs, in order

Commercial, first. Closing profitable revenue in a room where you already have standing, institutional finance, real assets (towers, REITs, energy), gov-adjacent (economic development, public agencies, policy), or engineering and professional services. Nobody sells credibly to all four, which is why this is several seats over time, not one. And the room you own today doesn't have to be one of these, the person in the first founding conversation came from none of them, the domain gets absorbed, the ability to close doesn't. That first conversation is well down the road, with someone who read the whole model and pushed back on the parts that deserved it. This page opens the next seat.

Platform and pipeline engineering, a close second. The substrate everything rides on, crawling, classification, data layers, scale and reliability. The entire capital-efficiency bet lives in this function, and the person who owns it earns the same way a closer does, on a build track with real numbers, not as support staff for sales.

Why you should probably not want this

Worth saying at full strength, because the filter is the point. Most good people should read this page and walk away, and that's the design working.

  • Encoding is the job, not the paperwork after the job. You have to be comfortable capturing your thinking, type it, speak it, draw it, interpretive dance if that works, the medium doesn't matter. What matters is that how you think about your problem domain gets encoded into the system itself, because that's what compounds. Experience that stays in your head never enters the system, and here, what doesn't enter the system isn't needed. If that feels like giving something away, or systems thinking isn't how your head already works, every single day here will grate.
  • The system watches, and it pushes back. Your decisions are tracked, your overrides are logged, and the operating layer will challenge you when you drift from a goal you set. It's designed to be adversarial the way a good partner is. Some people find that thrilling. Most find it insufferable, and they're not wrong, it's just not for them.
  • There is no empire to build. No headcount to accumulate, no org to manage into existence, no title theater. Empire-building is disqualifying here, not rewarded.
  • Your rolodex stops being your leverage. The relationships you bring get institutionalized into the company, on the company's contracts, multi-threaded, documented. You are paid well for exactly that, in equity and advancement, but if "my book is my power" is your identity, this will feel like a loss every week.
  • Nobody hands you the script. No manager, no enablement deck, no direction. The company coordinates through shared goals and encoded rules you help write, and inside your own domain, you're the one writing the playbook. If you need the script, this isn't for you.
  • And you don't hand out scripts either. If what you enjoyed about leadership was telling your team what to do, this isn't for you. Leading here means articulating the outcome and why, setting constraints, boundaries, and guardrails, and letting the system and the people run. That's how I direct the machine, and it's how partners direct everything.
  • This isn't the AI red pill. I've seen blockchain, the fediverse, the metaverse, every wave of true believers. I'm not asking you to become an AI acolyte, and honestly, if you are one, you won't make a good partner. AI is a tool, a really powerful one, not a panacea and not a godlike being that will rule us all (for now?). If you think you can just point "the AI" at it and everything will just work out, probably not a fit. The system works because judgment, rules, and feedback loops direct the tool, and that discipline is the job.
  • And a humility check. If you've never experienced imposter syndrome, if you've never heard pushback from your direct reports, if being challenged with respect is something you struggle with, probably not a fit. Everything here challenges you, the system by design, the people by culture. The flip side is the reward: if you're self-aware enough to know your limitations, you can design around them here, encode guardrails for your own weak spots and let the system augment your strengths. That's not a metaphor, it's how this company already runs, my own failure modes have rules with their names on them.
  • Confidence and ignorance can share a sentence. The job needs both: quote the price with conviction, and say "I don't know, I'll find out" in the same meeting without losing the room. If you can only do one of those, probably not a fit.
  • Silence doesn't need to be filled.
  • Match the pace of your partners. Or at least understand they have different pacing, and that not everyone comprehends things the same way, or in the same time. Buyers, partners, towns, none of them move on your clock. Some need to read it, some need to hear it, some need to sit with it, and reading that is part of the job.
  • Friction can be useful, negative friction can't. Challenge, pushback, adversarial review, that friction is how everything here improves, bring it. Politics, ego battles, resistance that carries no information, that's negative friction, and I can't tolerate it. At twenty people max there's nowhere for it to hide.
  • Being right isn't the job. Sometimes letting someone fail and improve is worth more to the system than winning the argument. The whole model runs on growing the people beneath you, promotion here literally requires it, so if you optimize for being the smartest person in the room, probably not a fit.
  • This isn't Amazon culture, this isn't FAANG culture, and it isn't outwork your partner. The system does the leverage, so hours aren't the currency here, encoded judgment is. Partners hold complementary spikes, you're not competing with the person next to you, and a business built to run forever can't run on burnout. Sustainable pace is a design constraint, not a perk.
  • Boring businesses are beautiful. This isn't about flash. If you need external validation, from VCs, peers, the industry, probably not a fit. Nobody here will ever brag about how much we raised or what we sold for. Money raised can be a vanity metric, headcount can be a vanity metric, having an assistant can be a vanity metric, and this company doesn't run on vanity. The scoreboard is durability, profitability, and sustainability, not a lottery ticket that pays out lower than you think after the dilution. Money talks, wealth whispers. This is the whisper, in company form.
  • And there's no base salary yet. Stated above, worth repeating in the list of reasons to walk away.

If you read all of that and got more interested instead of less, that reaction is rare, and it's the actual qualification.

And what's left, once none of that scared you off, is the culture, and the closest word for it is Swedish: lagom. Not too much, not too little, the right amount. The folk story says it comes from laget om, "around the team," the horn passed around the circle where everyone drinks just enough that the whole team gets a share. That's this company's economic design in one word: the partner pool, a founder share that deliberately glides down as revenue grows, a cap on my own pay, a company allowed to plateau on purpose once the machine is good enough. Enough, shared around the circle, indefinitely. Swedish colleagues I worked with for years called me the most non-American American they'd met, or on good days the most Swedish one, so this isn't a borrowed aesthetic, it's how I already operate. And the first partner conversation, well down the road as I write this, is with a German living in Sweden, the best of both, he'll keep me honest about the word.

The shape of the deal

Plainly, there is no base salary yet, and I don't pay myself one either. A base switches on for the whole team at our first revenue milestone, and the person who closes that revenue is the person who turns everyone's base on. You earn your own base here, the way a founder does.

  • Equity is earned continuously and locked once earned. No cliffs that move, no KPI re-gating, no clawbacks. I have watched earned equity evaporate behind shifting goalposts at a previous company, and this model is built so that can never happen here.
  • Earlier means more. Founding-stage joiners earn at a real risk multiplier, because taking the no-base stretch is the risk, and it's rewarded directly.
  • Commission from the first close. Self-funding, paid out of revenue you generate.
  • The return is yield. Partners share in annual profit distributions. Departing partners are bought out at a fair formula. Nobody needs the company to sell for the equity to be worth something.
  • Partner, not employee. You contract B2B through your own entity, in your own country, controlling your own taxes and benefits.
  • The full model is written down. Every number, every mechanism, every open question, in a working draft I share whole in the first conversation. You get the actual internal document, not a pitch.

Before you write, all of these should be true

  • You own one of the seats above. For commercial, a room where people take your call and you can prove it, and it doesn't have to be one of my four verticals, domains can be absorbed, standing and closing can't be faked. For platform, systems you have personally built and run at scale, that stayed up, that you can walk me through.
  • You have personal runway. The base switches on at a revenue milestone, and until then commission is the only cash. This has to work for your life, honestly, before it can work for the company. And the reason there's no base yet is the same reason there's no board: no outside money. Take investment and sure, you get a base for a while, but it comes with strings, and this whole model exists to never have those strings.
  • You'll work through your own legal entity as a genuine partner, not an employee with a different label.
  • You want durable ownership and yield, not an exit bet.
  • Reading how this is built made you curious, not defensive, and you'd rather compound a system than run your own playbook inside one.
