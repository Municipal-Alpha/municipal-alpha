Auburn wrote your pitch, four years early

The best single document in the set is a Capital Improvement Program worksheet. Auburn’s Recreation and Sports Facilities department asked for registration software and justified it like this:

Department: Recreation & Sports Facilities Project Title: Recreation registration and website software (RecTrac) Justification: ... The department wants to move from paying for five (5) different software systems to one (1) system. Cost FY2021 $25,000 Useful Life: 10 Yrs Priority: High Project Purpose: Improve efficiency

A Maine city of 24,000 wrote your consolidation argument about itself, put a high priority on it, and attached the largest recreation-software figure anywhere in the slice we can read. That is a request rather than an award, and the request is four years old, so treat it as a statement of appetite rather than a live contract. The appetite is the part that matters.

343 programs across two lines, 22,135 residents, and 45 waitlisted camp registrations. That is the dimension you said is usually invisible, and it is the one I can read straight out of a town’s own budget book where the department publishes it.

Orono publishes the same shape at a smaller scale in its annual report: “another significant increase to a new max amount of 40 students and a short waitlist of interested families in the After School Program”, and “237 participants enjoyed fall youth programming”. Waitlists are the cleanest version of your argument, because they are documented unmet demand written down by the town.

Where this stops

Four limits, because you will find them anyway and it is cheaper if you find them here.

Renewal timing
You said renewal dates would help and I agreed on the call. They are not typically in what towns publish. When a town does publish it I collect it, and when it does not I ask: 17 of the 33 records requests I have filed in Maine have come back, and the Falmouth term window above came out of one of them. So it is obtainable, on a clock of weeks rather than an afternoon. It is its own piece of work rather than part of the screen, and worth talking about once you have seen the screen.
Vendor names collide
Searching “Active Network” returns four Maine towns. Three of them are the ordinary English phrase “an active network of”: 150 partner agencies in a Westbrook grant narrative, pedestrian and transit connections in a Scarborough plan, 380 volunteers in a letter to a Cape Elizabeth library committee. One is the vendor. Every count on this page was read before it was counted.
Verbs follow the document
A municipal agenda states the intended disposition in the item title, so agenda-sourced items read like completed decisions. Auburn requested $25,000. Belfast’s $4,200 sits in a manager’s budget proposal. Neither is an award, and we gate that distinction rather than trusting the reader to catch it.
Revenue share
You raised revenue share as one monetization path. Searching for it does not work in municipal records. “Revenue sharing” returns 954 Maine documents and the largest identifiable use is Maine’s own State Municipal Revenue Sharing, a budget transfer from the state to the town, named outright in 333 of them. The narrower “revenue share” appears in 48 documents across 6 towns. The workable version is to find the vendor first and read the deal shape out of how the town books it.

One more, on the shape of this slice. 74 Maine documents mention any recreation-registration term at all, across 23 towns. Only 2 of the 74 are check registers, which is unusual and good: this is deliberative and budget material rather than a pile of a town paying its own bill. It also means the population is small, so nobody should promise you thirty towns of this quality in Maine at today’s coverage.
